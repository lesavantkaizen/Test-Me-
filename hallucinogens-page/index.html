What Are Hallucinogens?

Substances that alter perception, mood, and thought

Hallucinogens change how the brain processes sensory information, producing distorted perceptions, altered sense of time, and experiences that range from euphoric to terrifying. The category includes classic psychedelics — LSD, psilocybin (“magic mushrooms”), and DMT — alongside dissociatives like PCP and ketamine.

While most hallucinogens don’t produce physical dependence the way opioids or stimulants do, misuse carries real psychological risks: dangerous behavior while impaired, lasting perceptual changes, and severe reactions in vulnerable individuals. Ketamine and PCP can additionally drive compulsive, repeated use.

Signs & Symptoms

Signs of hallucinogen use

Physical Symptoms
  • Dilated pupils
  • Elevated heart rate and blood pressure
  • Impaired coordination
  • Numbness or detachment (dissociatives)
  • Nausea
Psychological Symptoms
  • Visual or auditory distortions
  • Altered sense of time
  • Confusion or disorientation
  • Panic or paranoia during “bad trips”
  • Detachment from reality
Behavioral Symptoms
  • Impaired judgment and risky behavior
  • Long unexplained absences
  • Declining performance
  • Using in escalating amounts (ketamine, PCP)
  • Withdrawal from usual activities
Risks of Long-Term Use

Risks of hallucinogen misuse

The biggest dangers aren’t always during the trip — some arrive after:

Impaired Judgment

Accidents and dangerous behavior while perception is distorted are the most immediate risk.

HPPD

Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder — visual disturbances and flashbacks that continue long after use stops.

Panic Reactions

“Bad trips” can produce overwhelming fear, paranoia, and lasting anxiety.

Psychosis in Vulnerable Individuals

Hallucinogens can trigger or worsen psychotic disorders in people with personal or family risk factors.

Dissociative Harms

Heavy ketamine use damages the bladder and urinary tract; PCP is linked to violent agitation and injury.

Life Disruption

Escalating use patterns crowd out relationships, work, and daily functioning.

A Common Question

Hallucinogens vs. therapeutic psychedelics

You may have seen headlines about psilocybin or ketamine in clinical settings. Research into medically supervised psychedelic-assisted therapy is real — but it involves screened patients, pharmaceutical-grade compounds, controlled doses, and trained clinicians in a monitored environment.

Recreational use has none of those safeguards: unknown dosing, unscreened risk factors, no clinical support during distressing experiences, and street supplies that may be misrepresented or contaminated. The existence of clinical research doesn’t make unsupervised use safe — and if your use has become something you can’t step away from, that’s worth taking seriously.

Withdrawal & Recovery

Withdrawal is safer — and easier — with support

Most classic hallucinogens don’t cause physical withdrawal, but stopping heavy use — especially ketamine or PCP — can bring cravings, low mood, anxiety, sleep problems, and lingering perceptual changes.

Treatment focuses on psychological recovery: stabilizing anxiety and mood, addressing HPPD symptoms when present, and treating the underlying reasons use became compulsive.

FAQ

Common questions, answered

Are hallucinogens addictive?
Classic psychedelics like LSD rarely cause physical dependence, but ketamine and PCP can drive compulsive use — and any hallucinogen can become psychologically entangled with escape and avoidance.
What is HPPD?
Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder — ongoing visual disturbances (halos, trails, static) and flashbacks that persist after use stops. It’s treatable with professional support.
Aren’t psychedelics used in therapy now?
Clinical research uses screened patients, controlled doses, and trained clinicians in monitored settings. Recreational use has none of those safeguards and carries real risks.
Can one bad trip cause lasting problems?
For some people, yes — particularly anxiety, panic symptoms, or perceptual changes. These respond well to treatment.
When does hallucinogen use need treatment?
When use is escalating, interfering with daily life, worsening mental health, or you’ve tried to stop and couldn’t. A confidential assessment is the easiest way to find out.
